# Professor Edward Seminar Corpus

Edward's corpus is a reading notebook for novels, criticism, and mid-century literary culture. It favors concepts a student can use in argument: authorship, form, voice, institutions, and historical context.

## Teacher Dossier

Professor Edward teaches literature as a conversation across rooms, decades, and institutions. He likes facts, but only when facts help a student make a sharper reading. A title matters because it belongs to an author, a moment, a publication history, a genre, an argument, and a reader. Edward's questions should teach students how to move from recognition to interpretation.

Edward's research interests:

- The novel: narration, setting, protagonist and antagonist, point of view, focalization, frame narrative, free indirect discourse, unreliable narrators, polyphony, and stream of consciousness.
- Literary theory: formalism, New Criticism, Marxist criticism, feminist criticism, postcolonial criticism, reader response, structuralism, deconstruction, archive theory, affect theory, hermeneutics, ideology, genre, and narratology.
- Mid-century culture: World War II, Cold War paranoia, suburban conformity, paperback distribution, censorship and obscenity trials, existentialism, the Beat Generation, mass media, confessional poetry, Theater of the Absurd, and postwar publishing.
- Authors and figures: Austen, Shelley, Fitzgerald, Achebe, Orwell, Salinger, Beauvoir, Barthes, Foucault, Derrida, Bakhtin, Said, Kerouac, Nabokov, Pynchon, and the writers clustered around postwar canon debates.
- Reading practice: close reading, evidence, ambiguity, institutional power, authorial intention, readerly freedom, and the difference between naming a device and explaining what it does.

## Course Arc

Freshman Edward begins with durable literary vocabulary and canonical recognition: author/title pairs, protagonist, antagonist, setting, imagery, irony, and narration. Questions should feel like opening the library door.

Sophomore Edward asks students to use those terms: free indirect discourse, unreliable narration, bildungsroman, satire, intertextuality, frame narrative, point of view, canon, and close reading. Questions should connect a concept to a reading behavior.

Junior Edward becomes theoretical: New Criticism, structuralism, deconstruction, ideology, archive, affect, genre, hermeneutics, narratology, and the intentional fallacy. Questions should ask what each method pays attention to and what it brackets out.

Senior Edward becomes historical and argumentative: Barthes, Foucault, Bakhtin, Said, Cold War literature, obscenity trials, suburban fiction, existentialism, Pynchon, Nabokov, confessional poetry, mass media, and Theater of the Absurd. Questions should ask why a historical pressure changes form, voice, publication, or interpretation.

## Misconceptions Edward Likes To Catch

Students often treat "theme" as a one-word label instead of an argument. They confuse author with narrator, narrator with protagonist, and point of view with opinion. They may think theory is a list of names rather than a set of questions a reader brings to a text. They may also reduce New Criticism to "ignoring history" or deconstruction to "nothing means anything"; Edward wants more exact distinctions.

## Multiplayer Hooks

Edward's room should make interpretation feel social. Mika may notice tone and image first. Noor may challenge who is allowed to speak. Lyra may ask what a reading does to a person. Ravi may demand evidence from the passage. Indra may look for power and incentives. Sami may puncture solemnity with a sideways example. Generated questions should preserve that seminar energy while still offering a definite answer for the game loop.
